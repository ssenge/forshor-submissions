\section*{Objective}
Full order-finding board: minimise $Q \times T$ (live-qubit peak times single-run T count) of Shor's
order-finding circuit at $n = 2048$, $\delta = 0.01$, on the IR ForShor prints under \texttt{--balanced}.

\section*{Starting point and credits}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Base policy:} \texttt{gghoryafi\_sub3} (pull request \#120), rank 1 of the full order-finding board
        on 2026-11-18. Fourteen of the sixteen bands, and every table except the $k=5$ table, are taken from it unchanged.
  \item \textbf{$k=5$ table:} the $k=5$ table of \texttt{npdeep\_sub1} (pull request \#107), points
        $0, \infty, 2, 1, -1, -\tfrac12, \tfrac12, \tfrac14, -\tfrac14$ (consumption order), with one inserted
        pair of operations (below). Its points and the rest of its program are unchanged.
  \item \textbf{Shift-pair padding:} cf.\ Suresh, Courtney, Tao and Cao (2026), Appendix B, Theorem B.3
        (two-integer profile): adding shift-left/shift-right pairs to a table raises its width profile to a larger
        target at unchanged cost.
\end{itemize}
All reused policies are Apache 2.0 board submissions.

\section*{Construction}
Rank 1's main multiplication (operands padded to 2348 bits) splits into $k = 5$ pieces, then 4, then 4:
\[ 2348 \xrightarrow{k=5} 481 \xrightarrow{k=4} 125 \xrightarrow{k=4} 36 \text{ (leaf)}. \]
The child width of a level is the least register width at which every value the table's program ever holds fits.
With the allowed points (0, $\infty$, $\pm 2^e$, $\pm 1/2^e$), the narrowest $k=5$ child at 2348 is 479, and the
next possible width from points alone is 481: no point set gives 480.

\paragraph{Why not 479 (the divisibility trap).} The next level cuts its operand into 4 pieces of $\lfloor w/4 \rfloor$
bits, and the top piece takes the remainder. At $w = 481$ the pieces are $120, 120, 120, 121$; at $w = 479$ they are
$119, 119, 119, 122$. The larger top piece makes the next child 126 instead of 125, which raises T by about 1.3\%
and cancels the gain. A narrower width is not automatically better.

\paragraph{Why 480.} At $w = 480$ the pieces are four even 120-bit limbs, so the next child stays at 125 and the leaf
at 36, while the top level's padding falls from $5 \cdot 481 - 2348 = 57$ to $5 \cdot 480 - 2348 = 52$ bits.

\paragraph{How 480 is reached.} Into \texttt{npdeep\_sub1}'s $k=5$ program (whose own child at 2348 is 479) we insert
\[ \texttt{["shiftL", 2, 3]},\ \texttt{["shiftR", 2, 3]} \]
before its 8th operation (index 7). Register 2 briefly holds its row times 8, then returns exactly; the program, its
points and its result are unchanged, but the visited row is now 3 bits wider, so the exact child width at 2348 is 480.
At the other widths this table serves: 588 gives 128 (rank 1's table: 129) and 551 gives 120 (unchanged).

\paragraph{An open note on what this does.} The submission format ignores declared widths and derives the exact
(least overflow-free) width from the program. Both exact-width definitions, the policy check's \texttt{tightWidth}
(\texttt{TableGeneration/RecursiveCost/BandedPolicy/Tight.lean}, over \texttt{reachRows}: every row of every visited
state) and ForShor's \texttt{exactSignedWidthOfWidths} (over \texttt{exactReachRows}), count the shifted intermediate
state, so the padded width is the certified one. In effect the pair declares a width of 480: a real circuit may always
use a register wider than the minimum, and shifts cost no gates.

\section*{Prices}
From \texttt{python starter.py price} (pack built 2026-11-12):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Full order finding: single run $T = 1{,}550{,}125{,}151{,}262$, $Q = 13{,}374$,
        $Q \times T = 2.073137 \times 10^{16}$; main ladder $2348{:}5 \to 480{:}4 \to 125{:}4 \to 36{:}\text{leaf}$
        (ForShor IR at exact widths, balanced split); end to end (386 trials) $5.98 \times 10^{14}$ T.
  \item For comparison, \texttt{gghoryafi\_sub3}: $T = 1{,}550{,}136{,}682{,}782$, $Q = 13{,}382$,
        $Q \times T = 2.074393 \times 10^{16}$. The change is $-8$ qubits and $-11{,}531{,}520$ T ($-0.061\%$ in the score);
        almost all of the gain is qubits.
  \item PhaseProduct board (2048, unchanged from the base): $T = 9{,}816{,}895$, $Q = 4{,}429$, ladder
        $2048{:}7 \to 312{:}4 \to 83{:}3 \to 31{:}\text{leaf}$; the two engines agree.
\end{itemize}

\section*{What did not help}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Recombining the 23 distinct tables of the seven board policies (local search over bands and a dynamic
        programme over one table per $k$ and the leaf cutoff): nothing below rank 1.
  \item Padding every other table: 1{,}431 padded variants (each visited row of each table, shifted by 1 to 8 bits),
        at every arity: only the $k=5$ padding at 2348 lowered $Q \times T$.
  \item Using the narrower 479 child directly (the trap above).
  \item Points $\pm 3$, $\pm\tfrac13$ would narrow some levels further but are not admitted by the table contract.
\end{itemize}
A lower bound (zero-cost table arithmetic, children at or above the narrowest allowed width, rank 1's arities) is
$Q \times T \ge 2.0034 \times 10^{16}$; with free arithmetic alone rank 1 would score about $2.0076 \times 10^{16}$,
so within this structure better widths leave at most about $0.2\%$.

\section*{What is not proved}
T counted in Python (Qualtran), not proved in Lean. Q is the live-qubit peak, a formula over the policy's widths,
not proved in Lean for this policy. The policy was priced locally with the starter pack only; the Lean checks were not
run locally. The board's own pricing is the one that counts. Search and pricing scripts were written with Claude Code
(Anthropic).
